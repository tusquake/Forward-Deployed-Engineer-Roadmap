# Inside an LLM: Tokens, Embeddings, Attention, Transformers, and Sampling

*A beginner-friendly, end-to-end guide to how Large Language Models read text and write responses*

## The Big Picture

A Large Language Model (LLM) looks like magic: you type a question and it writes an essay. Internally, it is a pipeline of surprisingly simple steps, repeated over and over:

1. **Tokenize:** chop the text into pieces and turn each piece into a number.
2. **Embed:** turn each number into a list of numbers (a vector) that captures meaning.
3. **Attend:** let every token look at the other tokens to understand context.
4. **Stack layers:** repeat the attending many times to build deeper understanding (the Transformer).
5. **Score:** produce a score for every possible next token (logits).
6. **Pick:** convert scores to probabilities (softmax) and choose one token (sampling, shaped by temperature).
7. **Repeat:** add the chosen token to the text and run the whole thing again for the next one.

**Analogy:** Think of your phone's autocomplete, but trained on a large share of the internet and powered by a far deeper understanding of context. Everything in this guide explains how that "super autocomplete" works.

---

## 1. The Core Principle: Next Word Prediction

### The fundamental goal

An LLM does one thing: **given the text so far, predict what comes next.** More precisely, it predicts the next **token** (a word or word-piece, explained in Section 2). To write a whole answer, it predicts one token, adds it to the text, then predicts the next one, and so on until it decides to stop.

**Analogy:** Autocomplete on your phone. You type "See you" and it suggests "soon" or "tomorrow." An LLM does the same thing, just with a much richer sense of context, and it keeps going for hundreds of words.

**Another analogy:** A friend who is very good at finishing your sentences. They aren't reading your mind. They are using everything they know about you, the topic, and how people talk.

### More context means a narrower prediction

The more context you give, the fewer plausible next words remain.

**Example 1: very little context**

> "I drink coffee every ..."

Possible next words: *day, morning, week, weekend, time, night, afternoon...* Many fit, so the prediction is spread out.

**Example 2: rich context**

> "I am a remote software engineer, and I drink coffee every morning before going to my ..."

Now the likely next words shrink to something like: *desk, laptop, home office, workspace, standup.* Words like "beach" or "school" become very unlikely.

The context ("remote software engineer", "before going to my") rules out most options and makes "desk" or "home office" far more probable than "office building" or "commute."

**Analogy:** The game of Twenty Questions. Each clue (answer) removes huge chunks of possibilities until only a few remain.

**Why this matters:** Everything an LLM does, from answering questions to writing code, translating, and summarizing, is built on this one skill: predicting the next token well, given context. The rest of this guide shows *how* the model understands context well enough to do that.

## 2. Tokenization: How AI Reads Language

### Why computers can't read raw text

Computers and neural networks only work with **numbers**. They can't do math on the letters "c-a-t." So before anything else, text must be converted into numbers.

**Analogy:** Morse code or a barcode. Humans read words; the scanner reads a numeric code that represents the product.

### What is a tokenizer?

A **tokenizer** is the tool that splits text into small pieces called **tokens** and assigns each piece a unique number called a **Token ID**. The model's list of all possible tokens is its **vocabulary**.

**Example (illustrative; real IDs differ by model):**

- Text: "I love pizza"
- Tokens: \["I", " love", " pizza"\]
- Token IDs: \[40, 3021, 18723\]

The model never sees the words. It sees only the list of numbers \[40, 3021, 18723\].

**Analogy:** A library. Every book has a catalog number, and the librarian (model) works with catalog numbers, not titles.

### One word does not always equal one token

Tokenizers often split less common or longer words into reusable **sub-word chunks**.

**Example (illustrative):**

- "cat" → \["cat"\] (1 token)
- "unbelievable" → \["un", "believ", "able"\] (3 tokens)
- "tokenization" → \["token", "ization"\] (2 tokens)

Pieces like "un", "able", and "ization" show up in thousands of words. By reusing them, the vocabulary stays manageable, and the model can even handle words it has never seen, by assembling them from known pieces.

**Analogy:** LEGO bricks. With a few hundred brick shapes, you can build almost anything. You don't need a custom-molded piece for every possible model.

**Practical rules of thumb for English:** a token is roughly 4 characters, or about three-quarters of a word. Numbers, code, rare words, and non-English languages often use more tokens per word.

### The trade-off: why not characters, and why not whole words?

There are two extreme options. Neither works well.

**Option A: one token per character**

- **Pro:** tiny vocabulary (about 100 to a few thousand symbols), and it can spell anything.
- **Con:** sequences become extremely long. "Hello" is 5 tokens, and a paragraph is thousands. The model has to process far more steps, which is slow and expensive, and individual letters carry very little meaning.
- **Analogy:** Writing a letter by sending someone each letter separately in 26 different envelopes.

**Option B: one token per unique word**

- **Pro:** short sequences, and each token carries meaning.
- **Con:** the vocabulary becomes enormous (every word form: run, runs, running, ran, plus names, typos, technical terms, and every language). Rare words are hardly ever seen in training, so the model learns them badly. A word that isn't in the vocabulary becomes "unknown."
- **Analogy:** A dictionary with a separate entry for every possible word, including every name, misspelling, and slang term. It would be impossibly thick.

**The sweet spot: sub-word tokens.** The vocabulary stays at a practical size (tens of thousands to a couple of hundred thousand tokens), sequences stay reasonably short, and rare words can be assembled from common pieces. A popular method for building this vocabulary is **Byte Pair Encoding (BPE)**, which repeatedly merges the most frequent adjacent pairs of characters into new tokens.

| Approach | Vocabulary size | Sequence length | Main problem |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Characters | Very small | Very long | Slow, little meaning per token |
| Whole words | Huge | Short | Unknown or rare words, giant vocabulary |
| **Sub-words** | **Moderate** | **Moderate** | **Best balance** |

### Tokenizers differ between models

Each model family trains its own tokenizer, with its own vocabulary and rules. So:

- The **same sentence can become a different number of tokens** in GPT-3, GPT-4, or Claude.
- The **token IDs are not interchangeable.** ID 3021 means something in one model and something else in another.
- Vocabulary sizes differ. For example, GPT-3-era models used roughly 50,000 tokens, and later GPT-4-era tokenizers used roughly 100,000.
- Newer tokenizers are often more efficient, meaning fewer tokens for the same text, which lowers cost and fits more into the context window.

**Analogy:** Different countries use different plugs and currencies. A ten-dollar note isn't worth ten in another currency, and a plug from one country won't fit another's socket. Token IDs are similarly specific to each model's "system."

**Practical tip:** Costs and context limits are measured in tokens, so use the provider's own tokenizer tool to count tokens accurately.

## 3. Vector Embeddings: Giving Words Meaning

### From an ID to meaning

A token ID like 3021 is just a label. It says nothing about meaning. Token 3021 isn't "closer" to token 3022 in meaning just because the numbers are neighbors.

So the next step converts each token ID into a **vector** (also called an **embedding**): a long list of numbers that represents the token's meaning.

**Analogy:** A person's profile on a rating site. Instead of just a name, you get scores across many traits: kindness 8/10, humor 6/10, punctuality 3/10. Two people with similar scores are probably similar.

### Meaning as coordinates on many dimensions

Imagine rating every word on many unseen "dimensions" such as *royalty, gender, food, technology.* Each word gets a score for each. The list of scores is its vector.

**Concrete (simplified) example** using four dimensions:

| Word | Royalty | Gender (male + / female −) | Food | Technology |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **King** | 0.9 | 0.8 | 0.0 | 0.0 |
| **Queen** | 0.9 | −0.8 | 0.0 | 0.0 |
| **Banana** | 0.0 | 0.0 | 0.9 | 0.0 |
| **Laptop** | 0.0 | 0.0 | 0.0 | 0.9 |

So the vectors are: King = \[0.9, 0.8, 0, 0\], Queen = \[0.9, −0.8, 0, 0\], Banana = \[0, 0, 0.9, 0\], Laptop = \[0, 0, 0, 0.9\].

### How AI understands relationships without knowing what words mean

The model never "knows" what a king or a banana is. It only sees numbers. But the numbers capture relationships:

- **King and Queen are close.** They share a high royalty score, and differ only in gender. The model can treat them as similar concepts.
- **Banana and Laptop are far from King.** Their scores sit on completely different dimensions.
- **Banana is far from Laptop** too: one is food, one is technology.

And you can even do arithmetic with meaning. The famous example: **King − Man + Woman ≈ Queen.** Take the king vector, remove the "male" direction, add the "female" direction, and you land near queen.

**Analogy 1: a map.** Cities that are close on a map are geographically related. Words that are close in embedding space are related in meaning. A mapmaker doesn't need to know what it's like to live in Paris to know it is near Brussels.

**Analogy 2: a supermarket.** Bananas, apples, and oranges are stocked in the same aisle, and laptops are in another building entirely. Position encodes relationship.

**Important honesty note:** In a real model, the dimensions aren't hand-labeled "royalty" or "food." Embeddings typically have hundreds to thousands of dimensions, and the model *learns* them during training. They are usually not individually human-readable. The labeled dimensions above are a teaching simplification of the same idea: **meaning becomes position in a high-dimensional space, and closeness means similarity.**

**Example of embeddings in the wild:** Semantic search. Searching "cheap laptop" can also find documents about "budget notebook computer," because their embeddings are close even though the words differ.

### One catch: embeddings alone ignore context

The embedding for the word "bank" is the same whether the sentence is about a river or about money. That is the problem Section 4 solves. (Note also that models add information about each token's **position** in the sentence, called positional encoding, so "dog bites man" differs from "man bites dog.")

## 4. The Self-Attention Mechanism (QKV)

### The flaw of reading words in isolation

Words change meaning depending on their neighbors.

- "I sat on the **bank** of the river." (land beside water)
- "I deposited money at the **bank**." (financial institution)

If the model looked up "bank" alone, it would get the same vector both times. It needs the surrounding words to adjust the meaning.

**Another example:** "The animal didn't cross the street because **it** was too tired." What does "it" refer to? The animal. A model that processes words independently can't tell.

**Analogy:** You overhear one word of a conversation: "bank." You can't tell which meaning is intended until you hear the sentence around it.

**Self-attention** is the mechanism that lets each token **look at all the other tokens** and pull in the information that helps clarify its own meaning.

### The QKV model: Query, Key, Value

Self-attention gives every token three roles, each computed from its embedding by learned math (multiplying by learned weight matrices). The easiest way to understand them is a **Google search**.

**The Google analogy**

- You type a **search query**: "best pizza near me."
- Every web page has **keywords/tags** that describe what it offers: "pizza, restaurant, reviews."
- Google matches your query against those tags to find relevant pages.
- Then it returns the **actual content** of the best matches.

Self-attention does the same thing, between tokens in a sentence:

- **Query (Q): what is this token looking for?** *Example:* the token "bank" asks, "Am I about water or about money? Which words nearby can tell me?"
- **Key (K): what kind of information does this token offer?** Like a page's tag or label. *Example:* the token "river" advertises, "I'm about nature, water, geography." The token "deposited" advertises, "I'm about finance, transactions."
- **Value (V): the actual information the token contributes** once it is judged relevant. Like the content of the page itself. *Example:* "river" contributes its water-and-nature meaning, which gets blended into "bank."

**Another analogy: a library.** The Query is your question to the librarian ("I need books on gardening"). Keys are the labels on the book spines. The Values are the contents inside the books. You match your question to labels, then read the books that matched.

### How the comparison works, step by step

Take the sentence: **"I sat by the river bank."** Let's compute the new, context-aware meaning of "bank."

1. **Create Q, K, V for every token.** Each token gets its own Query, Key, and Value vectors.
2. **Compare the Query of "bank" against the Key of every token** (including itself). The comparison is a mathematical similarity score (a dot product): the more the Query and Key point in the same direction, the higher the score.
3. **Get relevance scores.** For "bank" you might get raw scores like: I (low), sat (low), by (low), the (low), river (**high**), bank (medium, itself).
4. **Normalize the scores** with softmax (explained in Section 6) so they become percentages that add to 100%. For example: river 70%, bank 15%, sat 5%, others 10% together. These percentages are the **attention weights**.
5. **Blend the Values using those weights.** The new vector for "bank" = 70% of river's Value + 15% of its own Value + 5% of sat's Value + and so on.
6. **Result:** a **context-aware vector** for "bank" that now leans strongly toward "riverbank" meaning.

In the sentence "I deposited money at the bank," the Query of "bank" would match most strongly with the Keys of "deposited" and "money," and blend in *their* Values instead, producing the financial-institution meaning.

This happens for **every token at once**, each token running its own Query against all the Keys. (Technical note: scores are scaled down by the square root of the vector size to keep numbers stable, and in text-generating models a token may only attend to tokens *before* it, never future ones, since those don't exist yet. This is called causal masking.)

**Analogy: a group discussion.** Each person in a meeting (token) asks, "Whose input do I need to understand my own point?" (Query), glances at everyone's name tags and expertise (Keys), then listens more to the relevant people and less to the rest (weights), and takes in what they say (Values). By the end, each person's understanding has been enriched by the room.

**Example with "it":** In "The animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired," the Query of "it" matches strongly with the Key of "animal" (something that can be tired) and weakly with "street." So "it" absorbs the meaning of "animal." Change "tired" to "wide" and the model's attention shifts toward "street."

**Multi-head attention (briefly):** Models run several attention "heads" in parallel, each with its own Q, K, and V math. One head might track grammar, another references ("it" → "animal"), another topic. Their outputs are combined. *Analogy: several editors reading the same page, each checking something different.*

## 5. Transformers: Layering the Context

### Attention is one part of a bigger block

A **Transformer** is the full architecture (introduced in the 2017 paper "Attention Is All You Need"). It is built from repeated **Transformer blocks** (layers). Each block contains:

- **Self-attention:** tokens exchange information with each other (Section 4).
- **A feed-forward network:** each token's vector is then processed individually through a small neural network, adding further transformation and "thinking."
- **Supporting pieces** such as residual connections and normalization, which keep training stable and let information flow through many layers.

**Analogy:** A single block is one workstation on an assembly line, where first the team meets (attention), then each person works at their own desk (feed-forward). The full Transformer is many such workstations in a row.

### Why stack many layers?

Modern LLMs stack **dozens of layers**, some well over a hundred. Each layer takes the previous layer's output vectors and refines them further. Different layers tend to specialize:

- **Early layers: basic relationships.** Matching nouns to their verbs, spotting adjectives and the nouns they describe, simple grammar, nearby word links. *Example:* in "The red car stopped," early layers link "red" to "car" and "car" to "stopped."
- **Middle layers: richer patterns.** Phrase meaning, topic, sentence structure, sentiment.
- **Deeper layers: complex, long-range context.** Resolving who "his" or "she" refers to in a long paragraph, following a chain of reasoning, tracking a story across paragraphs, understanding the intent behind a question.

*(This is a helpful simplification. Researchers find that layers' roles are fuzzier than a neat list, but the general progression from simple to abstract is real.)*

**Example:** "Maria told Priya that his flight was delayed, so she called the airline. Later, Raj thanked her for the update." Figuring out who "his" refers to, who "she" is, and who "her" is requires combining many relationships built up layer by layer. A single attention step isn't enough, but many steps can do it.

**Analogy 1: reading and re-reading.** The first read gives you the gist. The second catches character names and connections. By the third or fourth, you understand the subtext and the foreshadowing.

**Analogy 2: image processing.** In a vision network, early layers find edges, middle layers find shapes, and later layers find faces. A Transformer does the same for language: from word links to meaning to intent.

### The final step: from understanding to the next word

After the last layer, each token position holds a rich, context-aware vector. To predict the *next* token, the model uses the vector at the **last position** (which has absorbed the entire prompt so far). Then:

1. This vector is compared against **every token in the vocabulary** (tens of thousands of options), through one more learned transformation.
2. The result is a **score for each possible next token**. These raw scores are called **logits**.
3. A higher logit means "this token fits better as the next one."

**Example:** For the prompt "I drink coffee every morning before going to my ..." the logits might be:

| Candidate token | Logit (raw score) |
| --- | --- |
| desk | 3.0 |
| office | 2.0 |
| laptop | 1.0 |
| ...tens of thousands more, mostly very low or negative |  |

Logits are not percentages. They can be any number, positive or negative, and they don't add up to anything meaningful. That's why we need Section 6.

**Analogy:** A talent show. After watching all the acts (context), each contestant (candidate token) receives a raw score from the judges (logits). The scores aren't percentages yet. Someone still has to turn them into a ranking and pick a winner.

## 6. Softmax, Sampling, and Temperature

### Softmax: from raw scores to probabilities

**Softmax** is a mathematical function that converts a list of raw logits into a **probability distribution**: every value becomes a positive percentage, and all the percentages add up to **100%**. Higher logits get higher probabilities.

How it works, in plain terms: take *e* raised to the power of each logit (this makes all numbers positive and amplifies differences), then divide each by the total.

**Worked example** using the logits above:

| Token | Logit | e^logit | Probability |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| desk | 3.0 | 20.1 | **66.5%** |
| office | 2.0 | 7.4 | **24.5%** |
| laptop | 1.0 | 2.7 | **9.0%** |
| **Total** |  | 30.2 | **100%** |

Now the model has a readable distribution: "desk" is the favorite at about two-thirds, but "office" and "laptop" still have real chances.

(Softmax is also what turns attention scores into attention weights back in Section 4.)

**Analogy:** Election results. Raw vote counts (logits) become percentages of the total (probabilities), so you can see each candidate's share at a glance, and the shares add up to 100%.

### Greedy decoding versus sampling

Once you have probabilities, how do you pick the next token?

#### Greedy approach: always pick #1

Always choose the highest-probability token. In our example, always "desk."

- **Pro:** consistent and predictable.
- **Con:** it becomes **robotic and repetitive**. Always taking the single most likely word tends to produce bland, generic text, and it can get stuck in loops ("I think that I think that I think..."). It gives the same answer every time, with no variety.
- **Analogy:** A person who orders the same dish at every restaurant, forever. Or a GPS that only ever offers the single most common route.

#### Sampling: a weighted lottery

Instead of always picking the top word, the model **randomly draws** a token according to the probabilities. It is like a lottery where each token holds tickets in proportion to its probability.

- "desk" holds 66.5 tickets out of 100, "office" holds 24.5, and "laptop" holds 9.
- Most of the time you'll get "desk," but about a quarter of the time "office," and sometimes "laptop."

**Why this helps:** Responses become varied and more human-like. Ask the same question twice and you may get differently worded answers. Over a long text, small variations compound into genuinely different, more natural writing.

**Analogy:** Choosing what to eat for dinner. You usually pick your favorite, but sometimes you try something else. That's what keeps meals (and conversations) interesting.

**Side effect:** Because sampling involves randomness, LLM output is **non-deterministic**, the same prompt can give different answers. This is also one reason hallucinations can never be fully eliminated: a lower-probability but wrong token can occasionally be drawn.

*(Related controls you may see: **top-k** keeps only the k most likely tokens before sampling, and **top-p / nucleus sampling** keeps the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities add up to p, such as 90%. Both cut off the very unlikely "nonsense" tail.)*

### Temperature: the creativity dial

**Temperature** is a setting that reshapes the probability distribution *before* sampling. Mathematically, **each logit is divided by the temperature T before softmax is applied.**

- **T = 1:** unchanged. The model's natural probabilities.
- **T < 1 (low, e.g., 0.2 to 0.5):** dividing by a small number makes the logits *larger* and the gaps between them *bigger*. After softmax, the top token dominates, and the distribution becomes **sharp**. The model is strict, focused, and predictable.
- **T > 1 (high, e.g., 1.5 to 2):** dividing by a big number shrinks the logits and the gaps between them. After softmax, the probabilities become **flatter**. Unlikely tokens get a real chance, so the model is more creative, surprising, and random (and more error-prone).
- **T approaching 0:** behaves almost exactly like greedy decoding.

**Worked example** with the same logits (desk 3.0, office 2.0, laptop 1.0):

| Temperature | desk | office | laptop | Behavior |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **0.5** (low) | 86.7% | 11.7% | 1.6% | Sharp. "desk" almost always wins. |
| **1.0** (normal) | 66.5% | 24.5% | 9.0% | Balanced |
| **2.0** (high) | 50.6% | 30.7% | 18.6% | Flat. Lots of variety. |

Same model, same scores, but the temperature changes how bold it is.

**Analogy 1: a thermostat for a crowd.** At low temperature, the molecules (tokens) settle into the most stable state, and everyone does the expected thing. At high temperature, they bounce around energetically and anything can happen.

**Analogy 2: a lottery with rigged or fair tickets.** Low temperature gives the favorite almost all the tickets. High temperature hands tickets out more evenly, so long shots can win.

**When to use which setting:**

- **Low temperature (0 to 0.3):** factual Q&A, extracting data, writing code, customer support answers, anything where accuracy and consistency matter. *Example:* a support bot quoting a refund policy.
- **Medium (about 0.7 to 1.0):** general conversation and balanced writing.
- **High (above 1.0):** brainstorming, poetry, fiction, naming ideas, where surprise is welcome. *Example:* "Give me 10 wild names for a coffee shop." Push it too high and the text turns incoherent.

## Putting It All Together: One Full Cycle

Here is the complete journey of the prompt **"I drink coffee every morning before going to my"**:

1. **Tokenize:** the text is split into tokens and converted to Token IDs.
2. **Embed:** each Token ID becomes a vector of meaning (plus position information).
3. **Self-attention (QKV):** each token's Query is compared with every other token's Keys, and Values are blended by relevance. "going to my" and "morning" and "coffee" now inform each other.
4. **Many Transformer layers:** the process repeats dozens of times, building from simple links to deep context.
5. **Logits:** the final vector is scored against the entire vocabulary, giving a raw score to each candidate (desk, office, laptop...).
6. **Softmax and temperature:** logits (divided by temperature) become probabilities.
7. **Sampling:** a token (say "desk") is drawn from the distribution.
8. **Repeat:** "desk" is appended to the text, and the whole process runs again to predict the next token, until the model signals it is finished.

**Final analogy:** An orchestra. Tokenization is handing out the sheet music, embeddings give each note its character, attention is the musicians listening to each other, the layers are rehearsals that refine the performance, and softmax with sampling is the conductor's final decision on the next note, with temperature deciding how daring the interpretation will be.

## Quick Reference

- **Core principle:** an LLM predicts the next token given the context. More context means a narrower prediction.
- **Tokenizer:** converts text to Token IDs. Sub-word tokens balance vocabulary size against sequence length. Tokenizers differ across models.
- **Embeddings:** vectors that represent meaning. Closeness in space means similarity. Dimensions are learned, not hand-labeled.
- **Self-attention (QKV):** Query = what I'm looking for, Key = what I offer, Value = what I contribute. Query-vs-Key gives relevance scores, which blend Values into a context-aware vector.
- **Transformer:** a stack of blocks (attention plus feed-forward). Early layers capture simple links, deeper layers capture complex context.
- **Logits:** raw scores for every vocabulary token.
- **Softmax:** converts logits into probabilities that add up to 100%.
- **Greedy vs. sampling:** greedy always picks #1 (robotic and repetitive), while sampling draws by probability (varied and natural).
- **Temperature:** divides logits before softmax. Low means sharp and predictable, high means flat and creative.
